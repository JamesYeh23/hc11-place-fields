## 1. Terminology primer

### Sharp wave–ripple (SPW-R)

One event, two measured components in the **local field potential (LFP)**, the low-frequency extracellular voltage at the electrode:

- **Sharp wave** — a slow deflection, ~50–100 ms, recorded in CA1 *stratum radiatum*. Dendritic signature of a large synchronous burst arriving from CA3 via the Schaffer collaterals.
- **Ripple** — the fast oscillation riding on it, ~140–200 Hz, recorded in *stratum pyramidale*. Generated by the reciprocal loop between pyramidal cells and parvalbumin-expressing basket interneurons, which chops the population burst into ~5 ms cycles.

**When:** during immobility and **non–rapid eye movement (non-REM)** sleep, i.e. when the **theta rhythm (6–10 Hz)** is *absent*. Theta dominates active running; ripples occupy the complementary state. This is why the paper's three epochs are PRE sleep, MAZE **immobility**, and POST sleep — even the MAZE events analyzed come from moments the animal stopped moving.

**Why they matter for plasticity:** ~10–15% of the CA1 pyramidal population fires in a single ripple, and a sequence that took ~2 s on the track is compressed into ~100 ms (roughly 10–20×). That compression is the point: **spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP)** only strengthens a synapse when pre- and postsynaptic spikes land within ~20 ms. At behavioral timescales cells A and C are seconds apart and invisible to each other; inside a ripple they are 20 ms apart and the synapse can change.

**In this paper the ripple is the unit of analysis.** Every "candidate event" that gets decoded is a detected ripple (detection in supplementary figs. S2–S4). Raw examples are visible in **Fig. 2C** (page 6): the wiggly trace at the top of each panel is the filtered LFP, the ticks below are spikes.

### "Coding properties" vs. "plastic properties"

The abstract's closing line says the two groups have different *coding and plastic* properties. These are two separate measurement axes:

| Axis | What it measures | Where measured | Figures |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Coding properties** | How well a cell represents space **during behavior** | On the MAZE | Fig. 3 |
| **Plastic properties** | How much a cell **changes** from PRE to POST | Across sleep epochs | Figs. 2, 4 |

**Coding measures:** spatial information per spike (bits/spike), within/outside place-field firing ratio, number of place fields, place field stability, spatial coherence.

**Plastic measures:** ΔPCC (change in per-cell contribution), change in within-ripple firing-rate gain, change in within-ripple burstiness, change in pairwise co-activation.

**Key structural point:** the rigid/plastic split is the **independent variable** in Figs. 3 and 4. It was defined using *only sleep data* (ΔPCC from PRE and POST ripples). The maze coding properties in Fig. 3 were never used to define the groups. That is what makes Fig. 3 a genuine prediction rather than circular reasoning — say this out loud when presenting, because someone will ask.

## 2. Background: the problem the paper attacks

*(page 1, paragraph beginning "The restructuring of hippocampal networks")*

- **Place cells** — O'Keefe & Nadel 1978 (ref. 1). A CA1 pyramidal neuron fires in one part of the environment, its *place field*. On a linear track, cells fire in a stereotyped order A→B→C over seconds.
- **Replay** — Wilson & McNaughton 1994 (ref. 10). During post-run sleep the same A→B→C order reappears, compressed into ~100 ms. Standard interpretation (Buzsáki 1989, ref. 11; Girardeau et al. 2009, ref. 7): the hippocampus rehearses the experience and transfers it to neocortex; disrupting the events impairs consolidation.
- **The complication: preplay** — Dragoi & Tonegawa 2011 (ref. 18) reported that sequences matching a track the animal had **never seen** already existed in sleep *before* the experience. If the sequence is already there, in what sense was it learned? Silva, Feng & Foster 2015 (ref. 14) pushed back and argued preplay is not computationally meaningful.

So the field had two incompatible claims about the same kind of event.

**The authors' move:** stop treating the population as homogeneous. Page 1 argues that networks built from similar neurons are unstable (ref. 15), and that Mizuseki & Buzsáki 2013 (ref. 16) had already shown CA1 pyramidal firing rates span orders of magnitude in a **lognormal** distribution — most cells slow, a small tail fast.

**Hypothesis:** rigid preexisting structure and learned experience-dependent structure come from *different subsets of neurons*, identifiable from basic firing properties.

## 3. Experimental design

*(page 2, first full paragraph)*

Four rats. Silicon probes in dorsal CA1, well-isolated pyramidal single units. Three epochs per session:

| Epoch | Where | What |
|---|---|---|
| **PRE** | Room A, familiar home cage | Sleep, before any novel experience |
| **MAZE** | Room B, C, or D | Running a **novel** linear or circular track |
| **POST** | Room A, familiar home cage | Sleep, after the experience |

Eight novel exploration sessions, **491 place cells** total. Layout in supplementary fig. S1.

Two design choices worth pausing on:

- **The track is novel.** If it were familiar you could not separate "preexisting" from "learned." Novelty is what makes PRE a genuine before-baseline.
- **Sleep happens in a familiar home cage in a different room from the maze.** The sleep environment is constant across PRE and POST, so the only thing that changed between them is the maze experience.

The comparison the whole paper rests on is **POST vs. PRE**. Anything in PRE is preexisting; anything appearing only in POST is attributable to the experience.

## 4. Method 1: Bayesian decoding and the sequence score (rZ)

**Step 1 — build the template.** During MAZE running, compute each place cell's tuning curve (mean firing rate vs. position). Stack all cells into a matrix. **Fig. 1A** (page 5) shows this for one session with 77 simultaneously recorded cells; rows are sorted by field location, hence the staircase.

**Step 2 — decode.** Take a ripple, chop it into **20 ms bins**. Each bin gives a spike-count vector across cells. Ask: given this pattern, where would the animal have to be? Assuming Poisson firing,

```
P(position | spikes) ∝ P(spikes | position) × P(position)
```

The answer is a **posterior distribution** over positions, not a point estimate. Doing this for every bin yields a 2-D map: time within the ripple × track position, color = posterior probability. **Fig. 1B** shows 18 such maps (vertical axis = bin time in ms, horizontal = position in m).

**Step 3 — score it.** A real replay event shows decoded position moving smoothly and monotonically across time — a diagonal streak. Quantified as a **weighted correlation** between time and decoded position, weighting each point by its posterior probability. That is the `r` printed above each panel in Fig. 1B.

- **r > 0** → **forward** replay (decoded position advances in the direction the animal ran)
- **r < 0** → **reverse** replay (Foster & Wilson 2006, ref. 4; Diba & Buzsáki 2007, ref. 3)

**Step 4 — normalize to rZ.** Raw correlations are not comparable across events (events differ in duration and number of participating cells). Build a null by shuffling, then z-score the observed value against it. Formula printed on **Fig. 1C**:

```
rZ = ( |r(observed)| − mean|r(null)| ) / SD(|r(null)|)
```

`rZ = 0` is chance; `rZ > 0` means more sequential structure than shuffled data. **rZ is the paper's main dependent variable.**

## 5. Figure 1: the setup result (page 5)

**Fig. 1C** — cumulative distribution of rZ per epoch, inset shows means. **Fig. 1D** — forward and reverse events plotted separately.

Two findings:

1. **MAZE > POST > PRE**, and **POST > PRE is significant.** That is replay: experience increased sequential structure in subsequent sleep. Confirms the method works.
2. **PRE is significantly above zero.** Before the rat ever entered Room B, its sleep already contained ripple sequences decoding as trajectories through a track it had never seen. **That is preplay.**

Figure 1 therefore *sets up* the tension rather than resolving it. Both phenomena are real, in the same animals, in the same recordings. The rest of the paper asks **which neurons** are responsible for each.

## 6. Method 2: per-cell contribution (PCC) and the rigid/plastic split

This is the paper's methodological contribution.

**The problem:** rZ is a **population** measure. It tells you an event was sequential; it says nothing about any individual neuron.

**The trick — a leave-one-out style shuffle.** For each neuron:

1. Compute rZ for an event normally.
2. Recompute with **only that neuron's** place field shuffled, everything else intact.
3. The drop in rZ is that neuron's contribution to that event.
4. Average across all PRE events and all POST events **separately**.

Each cell therefore gets a **PCC score in PRE** and a **PCC score in POST**. The logic is a knockout: if removing a cell destroys the sequence, that cell was carrying it.

**Fig. 2A** (page 6) — PCC distributions by epoch, same cumulative format as Fig. 1C.

### Fig. 2B is the definitional panel of the entire paper

Scatter plot: x = PRE PCC, y = POST PCC, one point per neuron.

- **r = 0.58, p < 0.001.** Most cells sit near the diagonal — a cell contributing heavily in PRE contributes heavily in POST. First evidence of rigidity. Page 2 quantifies it: **73%** of neurons strongly contributing to PRE also contributed to POST.
- **X's vs. dots.** X's = "strongly contributing" neurons, i.e. statistically significant PCC in PRE or POST — **216 of 491**. Dots = weakly contributing, excluded from classification.
- **Blue vs. red.** Among those 216, compute **ΔPCC = POST PCC − PRE PCC**, then split at the **median within each session**:
  - **Bottom 50% of ΔPCC → rigid cells (blue)** — contribution unchanged or reduced
  - **Top 50% of ΔPCC → plastic cells (red)** — contribution increased

Geometrically, ΔPCC is vertical distance from the identity line: red above, blue below.

## 7. Figure 2: how much does each group matter? (page 6)

**Fig. 2C — qualitative illustration.** Six ripple events, two per epoch, corresponding to the top row of Fig. 1B. Blue ticks = rigid spikes, red = plastic, gray = other place cells, diamonds = each cell's within-ripple spike-time center of mass. Left to right: blue dominates in PRE; red appears in force and forms the diagonal in MAZE and POST.

**Fig. 2D — the strongest panel in the paper.** A knockout curve.

- y-axis: **ΔrZ = POST rZ − PRE rZ**, i.e. the replay effect itself (zero = no replay)
- x-axis: percentage of cells whose place fields have been shuffled out of the template

| Curve | Order of removal | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Blue** | Lowest ΔPCC first (kill rigid cells) | Stays flat and positive; replay survives removal of 75% of cells |
| **Red** | Highest ΔPCC first (kill plastic cells) | Collapses through zero and goes negative by **10–20%** of cells removed |

Both curves necessarily meet at zero at x = 100%. **The asymmetry is the result.** Page 2: replay was eliminated after shuffling or removal of the top 10–20% of cells with the highest ΔPCC, whereas it remained after removal of the bottom 75%.

**Fig. 2E** — same test by outright cell removal rather than shuffling, shown as bars for PRE, POST, and POST−PRE. Same conclusion.

**Thesis sentence of the paper (page 2):** from PRE to POST sleep, plastic neurons are **added to a preexisting backbone structure**, leading to the increase in maze-related sequential activity (replay) associated with learning. *Backbone plus added layer.*

## 8. Figure 3: coding properties (page 7)

Groups defined from sleep; properties measured on the maze. This is the prediction test.

**Fig. 3A** — six bar comparisons (blue = rigid, red = plastic). Note log axes on the top row.

| Measure | Result | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Within/outside place-field firing rate ratio | Plastic ≫ rigid (p<0.0005) | Plastic cells are quiet outside their field |
| Spatial information per spike (bits/spike) | Plastic > rigid (p<0.0005) | Each plastic spike is more informative about position |
| Spatial information per second | Plastic > rigid (p<0.05) | Holds on a per-time basis too |
| Place field stability (r) | Plastic > rigid (p<0.05) | More reproducible field across laps |
| Spatial coherence (r) | Plastic > rigid (p<0.05) | Smoother, less fragmented field |
| **Number of place fields** | Plastic **<** rigid (p<0.005) | Plastic ≈ single field; rigid have several |

Everything points the same way: **plastic cells are clean, selective place cells; rigid cells are promiscuous, firing in multiple locations with low selectivity.**

**Fig. 3B — the learning curve, the panel that earns the word "learned" in the title.**

- x-axis: lap number as % of session
- y-axis: % of each cell's spikes falling inside its place field (a running specificity measure)

| Group | Trend |
|---|---|
| **Red (plastic)** | Rises across laps, **r = 0.14, p < 0.001** |
| **Blue (rigid)** | Flat, **r = 0.02** |

Right sub-panel summarizes per cell. Page 2: precision in spatial coding is a property **developed during maze running** by a small plastic subset.

This converts a correlation into something closer to a mechanism — plastic cells are not merely different, they are *changing in real time during the behavior*, in the direction expected if they were acquiring the representation.

## 9. Figure 4: biophysical and network properties (page 8)

This is where "fast-firing" and "slow-firing" get attached to the rigid/plastic labels.

**Fig. 4A** — six cumulative distributions (blue = rigid, red = plastic; dashed lines = medians):

1. **Off-line (sleep) firing rate: rigid higher, p < 0.001.** Rigid = fast-firing, plastic = slow-firing. This is the link back to the lognormal distribution.
2. **POST − PRE change in overall firing rate: p < 0.96, no difference.** ⚠️ **Control panel — point at this when presenting.** It rules out the boring explanation that plastic cells simply became more active. Their *total* rate did not change; *where and when* they fire did.
3. **Within-ripple firing-rate gain: plastic higher.** Gain = in-ripple rate ÷ baseline rate. Plastic cells are preferentially recruited by ripples despite being slow overall.
4. **Change in within-ripple gain (PRE→POST): plastic higher.** Recruitment strengthened with experience.
5. **Change in within-ripple burstiness: plastic higher.** Bursts (multiple spikes at short inter-spike intervals) induce plasticity far more effectively than isolated spikes.
6. **Change in pairwise co-activity: plastic higher.** Plastic cells became more correlated with one another.

**Fig. 4B–C — a firing-rate analysis that abandons the rigid/plastic labels entirely.** Sort all cells by overall session firing rate (panel B left, showing the lognormal curve), then compute pairwise co-activation (Pearson correlation of firing rates in 100 ms bins) for sliding groups of 20% of cells with similar rates (step 1%). Plot as a matrix with firing rate on both axes:

| Matrix | Hot region | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| **PRE** | Upper-right | Preexisting correlation structure belongs to **fast** cells |
| **POST** | Similar, plus new structure | — |
| **POST − PRE** | **Lower-left** | The *increase* belongs to **slow** cells |
| **REPLAY** (partial corr. RUN↔POST controlling for PRE) | **Lower-left** | Maze-specific structure carried into sleep lives in **slow** cells |

**Fig. 4D–E — the replication that makes the whole thing robust.** Drop PCC entirely; split the 491 place cells by median firing rate (D) or median within-ripple gain (E), then redo the replay analysis:

- **(D)** Low-rate cells (median **0.39 Hz**) show a significant PRE→POST increase in sequence score. High-rate cells (median **1.12 Hz**) show none.
- **(E)** High-ripple-gain cells show the increase; low-ripple-gain cells show none.

**Why this matters:** PCC is a method the authors invented, and a reviewer is entitled to distrust a result that exists only inside a new metric. Panels D and E reproduce the core finding using an independent, assumption-light criterion — a median split on raw firing rate. Page 3 notes the result also held across several Bayesian and non-Bayesian replay methods (figs. S19–S22).

Mechanistic sentence following from Fig. 4E (page 3): PRE→POST increases in sequence content were limited to cells with high ripple-specific recruitment, suggesting that **ripples are privileged windows for learning-related changes in excitability**.

## 10. Conclusion (page 3)

The authors' own summary: sequences in a novel environment are formed from a combination of a relatively **fast-firing** group of pyramidal neurons with relatively unchanging temporal dynamics and a **slow-firing plastic subset**.

| | **Rigid** | **Plastic** |
|---|---|---|
| Firing rate | Fast (~1.12 Hz median) | Slow (~0.39 Hz median) |
| Place fields | Multiple, low selectivity | Single, high selectivity |
| Spatial info per spike | Low | High |
| Change during running | None (Fig. 3B, r = 0.02) | Improves (Fig. 3B, r = 0.14) |
| Ripple recruitment | Lower gain | Higher gain, increases with experience |
| Co-activation | High in PRE, unchanged | Low in PRE, increases into POST |
| Contribution to replay increase | Negligible | Carries essentially all of it |
| Proposed role | Preexisting backbone / **preplay** | Learned content / **replay** |

**Proposed functional division (page 3):** fast-firing neurons may **generalize** across situations, whereas slow-firing neurons may **differentiate** among them. Fast cells are the scaffold that keeps the network stable and gives any new sequence a coordinate frame to attach to; slow cells are the sparse, high-information code distinguishing *this* experience from every other one.

## 11. Significance

- **It dissolves the preplay/replay conflict.** Dragoi & Tonegawa and Wilson & McNaughton were both right, measuring different subpopulations with an analysis that could not tell them apart.
- **It gives the lognormal firing-rate distribution a job.** A descriptive fact becomes a functional architecture: a small fast core for stability, a large slow periphery for plasticity. Reported in cortex and cultured networks too (ref. 17), so it may be a general design principle.
- **It reframes what sparse coding is for.** Slow-firing cells have historically been the annoying ones — hard to sort, few spikes, often discarded. This paper says they carry the new information.
- **Ripples become a plasticity window, not just a readout window.** Fig. 4E predicts closed-loop experiments: selectively disrupting ripples should remove the plastic subset's contribution and leave the rigid backbone intact.
- **Practical consequence for decoding pipelines.** A decoder trained on all sorted units is numerically dominated by fast-firing cells carrying the *least* spatial information per spike. The units doing the representational work are exactly the low-rate units a sorter is most likely to miss, merge, or classify as noise. Cluster-quality thresholds have a hidden cost here.

## 12. Critiques to raise in discussion

- **Median split on a continuous variable.** The authors concede it in the abstract's final clause — the groups form a *continuous* distribution. "Rigid" and "plastic" are labels imposed on a gradient, not discovered cell classes. Fig. 4D–E partly defuses this by reproducing the effect with a rate split, but the dichotomy remains an analytic choice.
- **Regression to the mean.** ΔPCC = POST − PRE, and cells are classified *by* it. Any cell whose PRE score was low by measurement noise will tend to show a positive Δ. Shuffle controls are in figs. S9–S12, but this is the first objection a statistician raises.
- **Slow cells are noisier by construction.** Fewer spikes → noisier tuning curves, rates, and PCC. Some group differences could be estimation artifacts. The best defense is Fig. 3B: a pure noise story does not predict a monotonic trend across laps.
- **It is correlational.** No manipulation; nobody silenced the plastic cells and showed memory degraded.
- **n = 4 rats, 8 sessions.** Standard for the field in 2016, small by current standards.
- **The behavior is not a memory task.** Rats explore a novel track; there is no learning criterion and no recall test. "Learning" is inferred entirely from neural change. Strictly, the paper demonstrates experience-dependent neural plasticity, not memory.
